# Composing a coherent screen

Read the sections that match the composition decision being made or reviewed. Apply them to the rendered interface and its actual content; they are relationships to preserve, not a fixed visual style.

## Proportion and density

Size regions for reading, comparing, entering, or manipulating their contents. A short setting needs less width than a comparison table. App-shell width does not determine content width. Limit reading measure through the content column; that limit does not by itself justify another framed surface.

Whitespace should separate groups, support reading, or accommodate work and growth. If it serves none of these purposes, adjust the composition. Keep sparse datasets honest: useful content determines the occupied space. There is no universal occupancy ratio or ideal density.

Evaluate the whole control region at its actual container width. Group identity, object context, retrieval, actions, and status by scope before assigning rows. Sparse groups at the same level can share a row; separate rows need a task distinction or a real space constraint. Navigation and tools for the current object still need recognizable grouping when placed beside each other. Keep counts and status near the information they describe.

When space is constrained, wrap coherent groups while keeping important actions discoverable. Choose field and group widths from their contents and relationships; inherited full-width fields or arbitrary minimum widths can create unrelated gaps or orphan controls.

## Surfaces and boundaries

Assign roles before choosing treatments: page, content region, nested group, interactive state, and floating layer. Compare adjacent surfaces in every supported theme. A table header must belong to its table; a menu must read as a layer above its origin.

Spacing, tone, borders, and elevation serve different purposes. A divider separates neighboring content; an outline identifies a boundary; a shadow communicates depth. Keep the combination that makes the relationship clear. When nested containers repeat the same task-level role, share one surface and use alignment or spacing for internal groups.

When a useful divider feels too prominent, reduce its visual weight while preserving the grouping cue. Evaluate translucent edges after compositing them over their actual surfaces, including floating layers. Necessary control boundaries and focus indicators need their own treatment. Check interaction states against the surface they occupy: a state fill that works on the page may disappear inside a menu.

## Color and identity

Calibrate emphasis to importance, area, repetition, and contrast. Hue, lightness, chroma, and filled area are separate decisions. A filled action can use a soft color; its primary role does not require a dark or saturated fill. Choose a readable foreground for the intended fill instead of assuming every accent requires white text.

When a person approves an existing treatment, preserve its relevant visual qualities while adjusting the palette. Compare the result beside that reference and check that the action remains discoverable in context.

Keep sustained reading and writing on a quiet, usually neutral surface. Express identity in purposeful locations such as a workspace marker, selected object, or action. A colored reading surface needs a content or user need of its own. Color can identify ownership, categories, and meaningful states when those roles remain stable and have non-color cues.

Marketing, onboarding, and frequent operational work have different priorities. An expressive introduction may explain an offer; a daily workspace should prioritize useful material. Carry character through typography, proportion, color, and detail suited to the task.

In a family of products or workspaces, distinguish identity from feedback. An accent can follow a workspace through navigation, selection, content markers, and its dialogs while success, warning, and error retain shared meanings. Keep the interaction grammar stable and evaluate the amount and placement of color in the full composition.

## Component geometry and states

Reuse a small, purposeful set of type styles, spacing relationships, control sizes, radii, border strengths, and elevation levels. Treat default, hover, focus, pressed, selected, disabled, and invalid states as one component.

Choose control emphasis by function and context. A field may need a persistent edge; a named toolbar action or filter may be recognizable through its label, icon, and state treatment. Preserve discoverability and keyboard focus when reducing outlines.

Evaluate neighboring targets together: select one item, then hover or focus the next, including inside nested groups. Independent rounded fills need a consistent visible gap; contiguous rows and segmented controls need intentional shared boundaries. Preserve these relationships across states.

Choose shape by role, scale, and adjacency. A standalone action, an input, and a joined control group need not have identical rounding. Where nested contours track each other, derive their curvature from the actual inset and border; keep shared edges coherent.

Check icon proportions, visible letterforms, optical alignment, label baselines, and internal padding in the rendered result. Centering asset and line boxes alone may not align the visible shapes. Reserve consistent leading and trailing slots for mixed content such as icons, avatars, counts, and selection marks.

Keep target positions, text metrics, and component footprints stable across states. Changing weight can shift labels even when padding is unchanged. Inspect the visible silhouette throughout a transition: a newly painted edge, expanding shadow, or changed clipping boundary can make an unchanged target appear to grow.

## Composition seams

Treat neighboring regions as one composition at their shared boundary. Equivalent pane headers share a row and alignment rule even when one contains search and the other contains a title and actions. A divider has one owner and a deliberate endpoint. Shared alignment does not require a continuous stroke; an inset or no line may clarify a relationship better than intersecting rules.

Inspect junctions, doubled strokes, mismatched insets, and interrupted contours at normal viewing scale. Let shared layout accommodate wrapped labels, larger text, and missing secondary content. Align regions at the same level; genuinely different groups need not force every edge onto one line.

A collection's header, records, and footer should inherit its content insets; entering an edit state should preserve the text axis. Distinguish these outer insets from spacing between internal columns. Shared alignment should follow the composition rather than unrelated component defaults.

## Consistency across screens

Compare equivalent tasks side by side. Shared actions need a stable name, placement relative to their object, scope, state treatment, and feedback. Reuse the same composition for equivalent collection controls; change it when the work actually changes. A different data type alone does not justify a different search or selection pattern.

Compare the same object in different hosts. Presentation should change for a meaningful task difference. Reading an email and following a message thread may need different structures even when they share components.

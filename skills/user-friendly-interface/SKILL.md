# User-friendly interface

Start with what a person needs to accomplish. Make the situation understandable, the next action discoverable, and the result recognizable. Visual quality comes from a coherent composition and carefully resolved behavior, not from adding decoration to an unclear workflow.

This is an independent synthesis of product-design guidance. It supports judgment; it does not certify usability. Consult [sources](references/sources.md) when researching a decision or selecting a reference.

## 1. Establish the task and inspect the starting point

Identify the person, their goal, what they know, frequency of use, input method, environment, and successful outcome. Preserve established product constraints. For a small fix, investigate the affected interaction; do not turn it into a redesign. Ask only about missing information that would materially change the decision, and label remaining assumptions.

For an existing product, inspect the rendered interface and walk through the affected task before proposing changes. Capture the relevant starting state when possible. Describe a finding as **observable condition → consequence for the person → proposed correction**. Separate a demonstrated defect from a preference. A screenshot cannot establish unobserved behavior.

Interpret feedback through the underlying need. “Too empty” may mean that related controls are disconnected; it does not automatically mean adding content. “Too many lines” may mean competing boundaries; it does not mean removing useful separators. Distinguish the variable being criticized—such as hue, lightness, saturation, area, or spacing—from the interaction treatment the person wants to preserve. Verify the cause, apply the smallest coherent correction within those constraints, and check whether it resolves the original difficulty.

Stay within the requested mode: research supplies evidence, review supplies findings, design supplies a proposed experience, and implementation changes the product. A review request alone does not authorize a redesign.

## 2. Model the work before arranging components

State the scenario briefly: **person → situation → decision or action → necessary information → result**.

Distinguish the main workspace, its controls, supporting context, and global navigation. Every prominent section needs a role in this scenario. Merge duplication and remove sections that exist only to occupy space. Choose a structure suited to the work: comparison, exploration, editing, monitoring, or a sequence of decisions. Read [composition patterns](references/patterns.md) when choosing or changing that structure.

Choose references with comparable tasks, content volume, frequency, and devices. Borrow an explained principle, not an entire surface treatment. Reconcile findings into one visual and interaction language; do not assemble unrelated conventions from several admired products. Popularity and a polished screenshot are not evidence of suitability. Separate observed convenience from claims about retention: familiarity, social context, data, pricing, and switching costs may all affect continued use. Prefer mechanisms that reduce effort and support user control over mechanisms that create dependence. For each borrowed pattern, identify the task it serves, the mechanism that reduces effort, the conditions that make it suitable, and the interaction that will prove it works here. A reference list without this translation is not a design decision.

Before implementing a substantial change, state a short composition contract: the primary working area, the scope of its controls, the relationships that must stay aligned, and the states the change must preserve. Express these as observable outcomes, not adjectives such as “premium” or “polished.” Use this contract to compare the rendered result with the starting point.

## 3. Compose a coherent screen

**Hierarchy and proximity.** Establish a clear entry point, main content, and next action. Place labels, values, help, errors, and related actions together. Align meaningful edges and use a consistent rhythm. A collection’s header, records, and footer should inherit its content insets; entering an edit state should preserve the text axis. Distinguish these outer insets from spacing between internal columns rather than accepting unrelated component defaults. Common actions remain discoverable; disclose secondary complexity without hiding information necessary to complete the task.

**Proportion and density.** Size regions for reading, comparing, entering, or manipulating their contents. A short setting needs less width than a comparison table. App-shell width does not determine content width. Limit reading measure through the content column; that limit does not by itself justify another framed surface. Whitespace should separate groups, support reading, or accommodate work and growth. If it serves none of these purposes, adjust the composition. Evaluate the whole control region at its actual container width, not each bar in isolation. Group identity, object context, retrieval, actions, and status by scope before assigning rows. Sparse groups at the same level can share a row; separate rows need a task distinction or a real space constraint, not merely separate components in the implementation. Compactness must preserve scope: navigation and tools for the current object still need recognizable grouping when placed beside each other. Keep counts and status near the information they describe rather than distributing them to distant edges. When space is constrained, wrap coherent groups deliberately while keeping important actions discoverable. Inherited full-width fields or arbitrary minimum widths must not dictate unrelated gaps or orphan controls. Do not invent metrics, inflate rows, or add decorative cards to fill a sparse dataset. There is no universal occupancy ratio or ideal density.

**Surface hierarchy.** Assign roles before choosing colors: page, content region, nested group, interactive state, and floating layer. Compare adjacent surfaces in context and in every supported theme. A table header must belong to its table; a menu must read as a layer above its origin. Use spacing, tone, borders, and elevation deliberately. A divider separates neighboring content; an outline identifies a boundary; a shadow communicates depth. These cues are not interchangeable, and none should be mandatory everywhere. When a useful divider feels too prominent, reduce its visual weight before removing the structural cue. Judge translucent edges after compositing them over their actual surfaces, including floating layers; do not weaken necessary control boundaries or focus indicators along with decorative separators. Give each visible container a distinct task-level role. When nested containers repeat the same role, share one surface and use alignment or spacing for internal groups. Avoid stacking outlines, dividers, surface changes, and shadows that redundantly describe the same boundary; keep the combination that makes the relationship clear in every supported theme and state. Check interaction states against the surface they actually occupy: a state fill that works on the page may disappear inside a floating layer. Calibrate emphasis to importance, area, repetition, and contrast. Hue, lightness, chroma, and filled area are separate decisions. A filled action can use a soft color; its primary role does not require a dark or saturated fill. When a person approves an existing treatment, match its relevant visual qualities and compare the result beside that reference. Choose a readable foreground for the intended fill rather than assuming every accent requires white text. Preserve the requested action treatment when adjusting its palette, and check that it remains discoverable in context. Keep sustained reading and writing on a quiet, usually neutral surface. Express identity in bounded locations with a purpose, such as a workspace marker, selected object, or action. A colored reading surface needs a content or user need of its own; an available brand color is not that reason. A restrained interface can still use color to identify ownership, categories, and meaningful states. Give those uses stable roles and non-color cues; neither blanket monochrome nor unrelated colored panels establishes hierarchy.

**Component grammar.** Reuse a small, purposeful set of type styles, spacing relationships, control sizes, radii, border strengths, and elevation levels. Treat default, hover, focus, pressed, selected, disabled, and invalid states as one component. Choose control emphasis by function and context: a field may need a persistent edge, while a named toolbar action or filter can be recognizable through its label, icon, and state treatment. Preserve discoverability and keyboard focus when reducing outlines. Evaluate states across neighboring targets as well: select one item, then hover or focus the next, including inside nested groups. Independent rounded fills need a consistent visible gap; contiguous rows and segmented controls need intentional shared boundaries. State changes must preserve these relationships. Choose shape by role, scale, and adjacency: a standalone action, an input, and a joined control group need not have identical rounding. Where nested contours track each other, derive their curvature from the actual inset and border; keep shared edges coherent. Check icon proportions, visible letterforms, optical alignment, label baselines, and internal padding in the rendered result. Centering asset and line boxes alone may not align the shapes people see. Reserve consistent leading and trailing slots for mixed content such as icons, avatars, counts, and selection marks. Keep target positions, text metrics, and component footprints stable across states; changing weight can shift labels even when padding is unchanged. Inspect the visible silhouette throughout a transition as well as the layout box: a newly painted edge, expanding shadow, or changed clipping boundary can make an unchanged target appear to grow.

**Composition seams.** Treat neighboring regions as one composition at their shared boundary. Equivalent pane headers share a row and alignment rule; a divider has one owner and a deliberate endpoint. Shared alignment does not require a continuous stroke. Decide whether a divider is useful before refining its junction; use an inset or no line when intersecting rules imply a grid the task does not need. Inspect junctions, doubled strokes, mismatched insets, and interrupted contours at normal viewing scale. Let shared layout accommodate wrapped labels, larger text, and missing secondary content rather than tuning independent offsets for one screenshot. Align regions that represent the same level; genuinely different content groups need not force every edge onto the same line.

**Consistency across screens.** Compare equivalent tasks side by side. Shared actions need a stable name, placement relative to their object, scope, state treatment, and feedback. Reuse the same composition for equivalent collection controls; change it when the work actually changes. A different data type alone does not justify a different search or selection pattern. Compare the same object in different hosts, too: presentation should change for a meaningful task difference, not simply because another panel renders it. Conversely, reading an email and following a message thread need different task structures even when they share components.

**Appropriate expression.** Marketing, onboarding, and frequent operational work have different priorities. An expressive introduction may help explain an offer; a daily workspace should give priority to useful material. Carry the product's character through typography, proportion, color, and detail without forcing every screen into the same composition. In a family of products or workspaces, distinguish identity from feedback: a recognizable accent can follow a workspace through navigation, selection, content markers, and its own dialogs, while success, warning, and error retain shared meanings. Keep the interaction grammar stable. Recoloring every large region weakens hierarchy; evaluate the amount and placement of color in the full composition, not just a palette.

## 4. Design the complete interaction

Label actions by their outcome. Distinguish navigation, opening options, selection, and immediate changes. Give different effects distinct visible cues: selecting an item and changing its state must be distinguishable before interaction, without relying on tooltips. Use state indicators for their actual meaning: the current location, a selected option, completed work, and unread content are different states. Do not add a completion-style checkmark to every highlighted navigation item. Prefer the lightest clear treatment, preserve accessible state, and check that the open content establishes the current context. Operating a nested control must not also activate its containing row or card. Make primary, secondary, and destructive actions clear within each task; “one primary button per entire screen” is not a universal rule.

Check an action against the current state: **when it is relevant → what changes → how the person recognizes the result**. A control that looks actionable must have a meaningful outcome. If its goal is already satisfied, show the current state or omit a redundant contextual shortcut. Keep a disabled action when its stable position or discoverability helps, and explain what enables it. A validation action may need to remain available so it can explain missing input. Choose among these treatments deliberately rather than hiding every unavailable command. When a control disappears after use, preserve focus and the position of neighboring targets. Check equivalent states, such as any date within the week already displayed, not just exact value equality.

Provide feedback near the action. Cover the states the scenario actually needs: first use, no data, no matches, loading, partial results, error, permission limits, saving, and success. Explain recovery, retain input, and preserve context on return. Confirmations should reflect consequences; routine reversible work should not accumulate unnecessary steps.

Preserve continuity through the whole task: drafts belong to their conversation or object; changing a view changes representation, not the underlying records; return paths restore useful selection and position. Make recovery proportionate to consequences through undo, restore, or retained versions. Common actions should be discoverable before optional shortcuts accelerate them. Consult [familiar interaction patterns](references/patterns.md#familiar-interaction-patterns) when designing repeated operational work.

Keep the target under the pointer. Opening a menu, showing validation, or starting an operation must not unexpectedly displace its trigger. Motion should explain continuity without delaying an available action. Inspect both opening and closing, interruption, rapid repetition, and focus restoration. Respect reduced-motion preferences.

Give each bounded workspace a clear scrolling model. Keep ordinary reading in the primary flow; introduce independent scrolling only where it supports the task. Retain access to controls and context, make continuation discoverable, and provide enough inset at the end to read and operate the last item. Clipping a container must not make content or focus unreachable.

## 5. Adapt to people and environments

Preserve the task as available space changes. Base adaptation on the space left for the work after persistent navigation and supporting panes, not just the overall window. Reorder or sequence panels rather than shrinking the desktop composition. Retain selection, input, and return position. Check long content, localization, text enlargement, reading direction, safe areas, and the on-screen keyboard.

Account for applicable input methods: pointer, keyboard, touch, remote, voice, and assistive technology. Required actions cannot depend on hover alone. Provide recognizable focus, predictable traversal, adequate targets, and alternatives to color-only meaning. Essential labels remain readable; truncated values have an accessible way to reveal the whole value.

Use the target platform's accessibility requirements and conventions. Measure contrast for the actual adjacent colors and states. Necessary control boundaries and meaningful graphics have different requirements from decorative separators. Perceptually uniform color tools help tune a palette but do not establish readability or accessibility by themselves. Avoid arbitrary visual thresholds presented as scientific guarantees.

## 6. Verify the experience, not only the components

Before handoff, read [the review guide](references/review.md) and walk through the changed scenario in the rendered product. Inspect both the whole composition and component details, with representative content and relevant viewport, theme, and input variations. Compare equivalent regions at the same scale and measure their rendered alignment and dimensions when visual consistency is in question; matching style declarations alone are not evidence. Review the junctions between regions as deliberately as their interiors. Check shared components both in isolation and inside their actual hosts: an avatar in a card, an action in a toolbar, and a menu over a selected row can acquire conflicting presentation. A passing component example does not establish that the assembled screen preserves its geometry or hierarchy. Fix the cause at the responsible boundary, then revisit all affected uses.

Completion means the intended task works, the original problem is visibly resolved, related states remain coherent, and the evidence matches the claims. Report remaining uncertainty and untested conditions. Compilation, screenshots, and expert review answer different questions; none substitutes for observing representative users when actual usability needs to be established.

Keep transferable discoveries here and product-specific values, examples, and implementation decisions in the product's documentation. Add guidance only when it changes a decision or prevents a demonstrated failure; merge it with the existing principle instead of growing a catalogue of one-off fixes.
